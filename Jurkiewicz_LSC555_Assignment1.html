System Evaluation of the Smithsonian Digital Library

System Introduction

Context

The Smithsonian Digital Library (SDL) is part of the Smithsonian Institution, a nonprofit institution founded in the United States in 1846 that has become "the word's largest museum, education, and research complex." The SDL contains digitized items from the Smithsonian's exhibition and library collections, and the site is overseen by the Digital Library and Digitization Department, which manages book digitization for all of the Smithsonian Libraries and works on special digitization projects involving archival and manuscript material.

Users

The SDL provides free access to its digitized items for all members of the public, and therefore the contents are accessible to people of all backgrounds who can navigate the site in English. However, the target users are older adolescents and adults who seek to conduct research by accessing the digitized historical print and visual materials that are a part of the Smithsonian’s collections, especially in the fields of narrative history, art and art history, the history of science, natural history, literature, and anthropology.

Content and System Specifications

The SDL hosts over 35,000 digitized books and manuscripts, as well as digitized photo collections, ephemera, and seed catalogs. The main page contains three sections to search:

  • Books Online
  • Collections
  • Exhibitions
There is also a link to the Biodiversity Heritage Library, which had its contents digitized and was maintained by the Smithsonian Institution until recently.

Users can search the SDL catalog by typing a search term into the general search bar in the upper right hand corner of the page, then refine the search using facets. This acts a lot like a search box for a library database. Users who search this way will find entries for digital books and images integrated with other website content and also with records for content that is not yet digitized. Further browsing options are discussed in Interesting Features.

Most of the digitized books and images are in the public domain. Users have the ability to download all of the books as PDF and .epub files. Individual page images and plain text (uncorrected OCR) can also be downloaded.

Discussion of the System

Interesting Features

The Books Online page has two ways to browse the collection without starting with the search box. The first is to look at the page that shows the items that have been added in the last 3 months. This is an easy way to interact with the digital collection by casually browsing what has been digitized most recently. Another way to browse is to go to the page that categorizes online books by topic. This is a useful feature for users who are interested in exploring a particular subject but do not have a particular book author or title in mind.

Pros

  • ways to browse the digital books collection that are more intuitive for amateur users (most recent additions; topics)
  • a clean homepage with three distinct categories
  • discussion of copyright and the fact that most available material is not under copyright
  • ability for users to download practically all items

Cons

  • There are too many pages beyond the main page with information. It was difficult to determine exactly how many items have been digitized, for example, because this information appeared in a few different places and some of it is out of date (statistics from 2020).
  • The digitization staff and their contact information are a bit difficult to locate, as they are located toward the bottom of a large general staff page.
  • There is not a dedicated email for the SDL. I could only find a general submission form under Contact Us.
  • There are no foreign language options for searching or navitating the site.

Suggestions for Improvement

Some of the pages need to be updated, especially the Digital Library and Digitization Department page, which contains statistics of digitized items that are from fiscal year 2020. A few webpages about past exhibits that I found while searching the online catalog have image links that no longer work. This is to some degree inevitable with so much digital content, but staff should either try to keep links active or otherwise consider delisting defunct sites of lesser importance from public search.

Although the Smithsonian is a U.S. institution and therefore English is the appropriate language for the site, given the great number of Spanish-speakers in the U.S., it would be useful to have Spanish-language options for search instructions and search facets. This would make the site more accessible.

The digitization staff should be located at the bottom of the SDL homepage for quick reference instead of tucked away on the Digitization Department page. Also, there should be a public-facing email devoted to the SDL. As of now, one has to use the general query form. Even though there is an option to indicate the category of question, or which webpage is of concern, in my experience it is easier for queries to get lost using a general contact system.
